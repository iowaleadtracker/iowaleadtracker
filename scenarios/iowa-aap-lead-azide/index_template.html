Methodology and References

Background

The four cases. The Army estimates that 26% of the lead gets into the air with its mitigation (an emission factor of 0.26, in its Nov. 24 “Mitigated 0.26” maps), but no one has measured a blast like this. This map assumes about 9,700 poundsabout 4,400 kg (9,700 pounds) of lead azide in 68 drums (the amount an Army spokesperson gave), which the Army planned to set off in two blasts inside earth-covered bunkers at about 9 a.m. Wednesday (Army, Nov. 24). The Iowa Lead Tracker lets you simulate four possibilities for how much of the lead gets into the air: Low ($pct_low), U.S. Army mitigated ($pct_mid), High ($pct_high), or EPA permit ($pct_permit). In each case, the lead in the air comes in pieces small enough to breathe in, and the rest stays at the plant.

The low case comes from EPA and Army tests of military blasting caps, which use lead azide. In those tests, at least $pct_low of the lead got into the air, and almost all of the dust was small enough to breathe in. The high case uses a rule for explosions from a Department of Energy handbook. The U.S. Army mitigated case uses $pct_mid, the emission factor of 0.26 in the Army’s Nov. 24 “Mitigated 0.26” plume maps, for blasts in bunkers with added countermeasures (EPA’s permit uses 1.00 for all of the lead). The EPA permit case uses the 100% that EPA requires the Army to assume in its official plume maps; EPA calls that conservative. Different numbers would give a very different map.
